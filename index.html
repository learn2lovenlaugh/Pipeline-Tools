The area it assumes

Parabolic below z = 20, rectangular above

Original B31G idealizes the flaw as a parabola of area (2/3)dL, then switches abruptly to a full rectangle dL once z exceeds 20. That discontinuity is why long features get suddenly harsher treatment.

Guide

Reach for it
Legacy comparison, or where a procedure or consent order names B31G specifically. It is the most conservative method in this tool and nobody will argue you were optimistic.
Skip it
Essentially all routine work. Modified B31G supersedes it and is better calibrated to the same burst-test database.
Watch for
The z = 20 cutoff. Cross it and the assumed area jumps from (2/3)dL to dL with no transition — a feature can get materially worse from a small length increase.

Bulging factors compared

Q against M against Mt

Each method corrects for the remaining ligament bulging outward, and each uses a different curve. DNV's Q keeps climbing where B31G's M flattens, which is one reason the two diverge on long features.

Guide

Reach for it
Offshore and DNV-designed systems, where it is the expected method. Cases with combined internal pressure and longitudinal load — F101 has explicit provisions B31G lacks. And when you want sizing uncertainty handled explicitly rather than buried in a blanket safety factor.
Skip it
US onshore regulatory work, where B31G and RSTRENG are what reviewers expect. Also skip it on vintage or low-toughness pipe: F101 was calibrated on modern high-toughness line pipe and its use of SMTS assumes ductile, flow-stress-controlled failure.
Watch for
It keys off SMTS, not SMYS. Grades with a wide yield-to-tensile spread show a different answer from B31G for reasons unrelated to the flaw geometry. Part A also collapses toward zero as γd(d/t)* approaches 1 — real behaviour, but punishing on deep features.

Level 1 — screening curve

Rt against λ

API 579 asks a different question. Rather than a burst pressure it returns a remaining strength factor and asks whether it clears RSFa = 0.90. Your feature is the dot; on or above the curve passes.

Detailed calculations using actual inputs
Where MAWP comes from. API 579 does not produce a burst pressure the way the other methods do — it produces a remaining strength factor and applies it to a pressure you already have. By default this tool derives that pressure from the design equation, MAWP = 2 · S · tc / D with S = F × SMYS, because it is the only self-consistent value available from the inputs already on the page. That is a design MAWP on the corroded wall, not your recorded MAWP. If your line has an established MAWP — from records, a pressure test, or a derating — switch the basis to Entered on the Inputs tab and type it in. The reduced MAWP then scales from your value instead: MAWPr = MAWP × RSF / 0.90.

Looking down at the pipe surface

The block is the real corroded area. The hatched band is what B31G evaluates — the deepest point smeared across the full circumference, after which the width is discarded.

Axial slice — this governs

Hoop stress pulls sideways across this cut. Depth sets the remaining ligament; length sets how far it bulges before tearing.

Ring slice — width lives here, and is ignored

Widen the patch and this arc grows. The number above it does not move.

Why width never enters

Hoop stress is twice axial stress in a pressurised cylinder, so the pipe tears along an axial line — length controls how much the thinned ligament bulges, depth controls how much steel is left to resist. Every method here then assumes the metal loss already wraps far enough that no intact steel beside it shares the load. Width is fixed at its worst case, which is why a narrow groove and a wide patch of the same depth and length return the same answer. Recovering that margin needs API 579 with both longitudinal and circumferential thickness profiles.

How to read it

Immediate
ERF at or above 1.00, or depth at or above 80% wall. The allowable pressure has fallen to or below your operating pressure. Reduce pressure or repair — these are not next-run problems.
Scheduled
ERF between 0.90 and 1.00. Under 10% margin remains. Dig these on a plan rather than leaving them to grow into the immediate band.
Monitor
ERF between 0.80 and 0.90. Fine today. Worth watching if your growth rate is uncertain or the feature sits in an HCA.
The crossover line
Short features perforate while still holding pressure; long ones tear. A cluster of long features near the boundary is a materially worse finding than the same count of short ones, even at identical ERF.
What this does not do
Depth and length only, using Modified B31G. It applies no clustering — two features within 6t that would merge into one longer flaw are screened separately here and will read better than they should. Run the interaction rule on your tally before trusting the ranking.

Common ground

What all of them share

Every method here is a flow-stress-dependent plastic collapse model. They assume the pipe fails when the remaining ligament yields and tears, which holds for modern ductile line pipe and fails for low-toughness material. None accounts for crack-like flaws, and none uses width.

Where they all break down: pre-1970 low-frequency ERW, lap-welded, and some vintage seamless. In low-toughness steel, failure can be fracture-controlled rather than collapse-controlled, and every number in this tool is non-conservative. Those cases need a fracture mechanics assessment — API 579 Part 9 or a Level 2 FAD — not a flow-stress method.

Ordering

Expected conservatism

Roughly, most to least conservative on a typical long feature: original B31G, Modified B31G, API 579 Level 1, then RSTRENG and API 579 Level 2 (close cousins, usually landing together). DNV Part B floats depending on the grade's yield-to-tensile ratio.

If your ordering comes out differently, that is worth understanding rather than dismissing — it usually means the profile is doing something interesting, or the feature is short enough that the Folias curves have not separated yet.
